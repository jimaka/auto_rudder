Extremum seeking (ES) is a powerful approach to the optimization and stabilization of unknown
dynamic systems and is an active field of research in control theory. This paper surveys recent ES
algorithm developments. ES has evolved from its origins as a simple but brilliant engineering invention
in the 1922 paper of Leblanc to the breakthrough work of Krstić and Wang in 2000 in which the first
general stability analysis was given, to the most recent and numerous analytical results which prove
convergence for a wide range of general dynamic systems. This survey provides a thorough overview
of the dynamic systems for which ES stabilization and optimization results have been provided, the
various analytical methods for proving convergence, and an overview of many real world applications
for which ES has been utilized in industry and at scientific laboratories.

1. Introduction
Many real world control problems require optimizing the performance of time-varying and uncertain dynamic systems based
on measurable but analytically unknown output functions. Examples of such systems include maximal power tracking for solar
panels and wind farms operating in unpredictable weather conditions, anti-lock brakes which must compensate for unpredictable
and changing road surfaces, power management in large computer server farms with time-varying demand, autonomous formation control and collision avoidance in changing environments,
and the control and optimization of charged particle beams in
particle accelerators.
The problem of optimizing the performance of a dynamic system by tracking the extremum of its output function is known as
extremum seeking (ES). ES is a general problem and many methods have been developed over the years including well known
and established methods such as nonlinear programming (Teel
& Popovic, 2001). Various adaptive ES methods have also been
developed which utilize the knowledge of a system model with
unknown parameters and optimize system performance by using state rather than objective function measurements (Adetola
& Guay, 2006, 2007; DeHaan & Guay, 2005; Favache, Dochain,
Perrier, & Guay, 2008; Guay, Dochain, Perrier, & Hudon, 2007;
Guay & Zhang, 2003).

In this survey we focus on a perturbation driven method of
extremum seeking for optimization and control. This approach is
now ∼100 years old, as it was first proposed in the 1922 paper
of Leblanc (1922). In his paper, Leblanc reported on a new method
for maintaining maximum power transfer from a transmission
line to a tram car. Leblanc’s paper was a description of an ingenious engineering design, but there was no mathematical analysis
of the scheme’s dynamics. Eventually, Leblanc’s simple method
became a popular tool for maximizing or minimizing unknown
output functions of dynamic systems.
Various ES approaches utilize a broad range of advanced mathematical and control theory methods including singular perturbation theory, stochastic calculus, differential geometry, nonlinear
perturbation and averaging theory, weak convergence in Hilbert
spaces, and partial differential equations. Before surveying the
many recent cutting edge advances in ES, we start with a brief
description which provides an intuition which is the foundation
for all ES methods.

Leblanc’s simple and effective optimization scheme gained
attention in Russia starting in the 1940s (Kazakevich, 1943, 1944)
and in the U.S. the scheme was introduced in the 1950s (Blackman, 1962; Draper & Li, 1951; Tsien, 1954) for optimization of the
performance of an internal combustion engine. In the 1950s and
1960s the algorithm was modified and its performance for specific problems was analyzed (Meerkov, 1967; Morosanov, 1957;
Obabkov, 1967; Ostrovsky, 1957), but rigorous performance and
design analysis had not taken place. In 1971 a Lyapunov based
stability analysis of a particular system was performed (Luxat &
Lees, 1971), but general stability proofs would not arrive until
30 years later.
In 2000 the first general stability analysis of the extremum
seeking method was performed by Krstić and Wang (2000), by
utilizing an elegant combination of averaging and singular perturbation techniques. The work of Krstić and Wang sparked renewed interest in the field. Since 2000 the extremum seeking
method, as a real-time non-model-based optimization approach,
has seen significant theoretical advances (Ariyur & Krstić, 2003;
Choi, Krstić, Ariyur, & Lee, 2002; Rotea, 2000), with extension to
semi-global convergence (Tan, Nešić, & Mareels, 2006). In 2012 a
method of utilizing ES directly as a feedback controller was introduced for the stabilization of open-loop unstable time-varying
dynamic systems with unknown control direction and for optimizing their performance based on analytically unknown noisy
output functions (Scheinker, 2012; Scheinker & Krstić, 2012a).
Over the past 20 years a large body of work has been published
on various ES methods for systems of increasing generality. Although a few reviews of ES have been written in the past (Åström
& Wittenmark, 1995a, 1995b; Scheinker & Krstić, 2017; Tan,
Moase, Manzie, Nešić, & Mareels, 2010; Zhang & Ordóñez, 2011),
this survey attempts to cover the most recent developments in
this quickly growing field including both theory and applications.

For the ES methods described above, extending an approach
to multi-parameter systems is easily handled by dithering each
parameter with a unique frequency, resulting in a gradient descent in a high dimensional space, with minimal changes in the
resulting analysis. Beyond such a high dimensional ES approach,
a large body of work has focused on developing multi-parameter
ES in which the relationships between various tuned parameters
are studied in more complex arrangements such as cooperative
games or for Nash equilibrium seeking in noncooperative games
with limited game information.

15. Safe ES
Constrained optimization in unknown environments is often
performed through the use of Gaussian Processes (GPs), where
a measure of safety can be considered statistically. Safe optimization of quadrotor controllers is described in Berkenkamp,
Schoellig, and Krause (2016), and a general method for doing safe
optimization via GPs is given by Sui, Gotovos, Burdick, and Krause
(2015).
Several approaches have been developed for using ES to solve
constrained optimization problems, such as in Guay et al. (2015).
Their modified ES design also involves estimating the gradient of
the unknown functions, and they perform their analysis without
using classical averaging and singular perturbation theory. In Liao,
Manzie, Chapman, and Alpcan (2019) ES is blended with ‘‘boundary tracing’’. The formalization here is based on concepts taken
from nonlinear programming techniques. ES based algorithms
are presented in Poveda and Quijano (2015), which can handle
both equality and inequality constraints, inspired by evolutionary
game theory. A robust coordinated hybrid source seeking approach with obstacle avoidance has also been developed (Poveda,
Benosman, Teel, & Sanfelice, 2021).

Machine learning (ML) techniques, especially deep convolutional neural networks, have been growing in popularity in all
areas of science and engineering. However, one major challenge
faced by standard ML methods is that of time varying systems,
known in the ML community as systems with distribution shift.
If a system or its initial conditions change over time, then the data
that was previously collected to train an ML model such as a deep
convolutional neural network no longer exactly represents the
dynamic system that is supposed to be modeled. The typical solution to this phenomenon is brute force re-training, in which once
a big enough error is noticed an entire new set of data is collected
to update the network weights. This solution is problematic for
most complex systems of interest for which repeatedly gathering
new data and network re-training is time consuming and requires
measurements which interrupt regular system operation.
For example, at large particle accelerator facilities detailed
beam profile measurements use destructive processes such as
inserting screens which intercept the charged particle beams
and interrupt all downstream operations. Such measurements
are typically performed only during rare dedicated experimental
times. For such systems repeated re-training is not an option.

17. ES methods summary

One possible advantage of utilizing an ES-based feedback as a
controller directly is the added flexibility of finding and tracking unknown optimal points which may exist for analytically
unknown systems.
For example, if we consider the typical ES setup there is
usually a pre-existing controller α (x, θ, t) which guarantees the
existence of a smooth output steady state manifold l such that
the system equilibrium f (x, α (x, θ )) = 0 is achieved at x =
l(θ ) and the ES dynamics evolve along this manifold as they
find the optimal value of some J(x). For a large and complex
system with unknowns, which is the type of system for which
ES is most useful, it is possible that some other controller may
achieve a more optimal setting than what is constrained to l(θ )
by having the freedom to explore the space more completely.
Such an example is illustrated in Fig. 41 in which the classic
ES scheme quickly converges to and evolves along l(θ ) to the
global maximum relative to the l(θ ) manifold as enforced by a
pre-existing controller α (x, θ ). For this same system when using
the approach where ES is the direct feedback itself, from the same
starting conditions it is able to find the global maximum that is
outside of the l(θ ) constraints.

A large family of various ES-based controllers and optimization
methods has been presented for dynamic systems. In Fig. 40 an
incomplete summary of various ES methods is presented showing
the signal pathways and setups for the various ES versions with
the various methods broadly categorized in two families: (1).
Classical ES approaches and their modifications for the optimization of an analytically unknown output function of an a priori
stabilized dynamic system by tuning a control parameter. (2).
ES-based feedback stabilization and optimization approaches in
which the dynamic system is not assumed to be controlled and
ES plays the dual role of optimizing an analytically unknown
output function and of forcing the optimal point to be a stable
equilibrium of the dynamic system (see Table 1).
Starting at the top of the left column of Fig. 40, we have the
classical ES algorithm for which the first analytic proofs were
presented in Krstić and Wang (2000). As we continue down the
left column we see the various modifications and additions that
have been made to the ES method over the years including a
stochastic version, a multi-agent version for games, a Newtonbased ES setup, the extension of the Newton approach to systems
with delays and systems governed by a class of PDEs, finite-time
Newton ES, a hybrid ES scheme for systems whose dynamics are
a combination of continuous movement and discrete jumps, and
safe ES in which analytically unknown boundary constraints must
be satisfied by the ES dynamics while minimizing an analytically
unknown output function.
Starting at the top of the right column of Fig. 40, we have the
ES-based feedback control algorithm first proposed in Scheinker
(2012). As we continue down the right column we see various
modifications of the ES-based feedback approach including the
bounded form of ES, the PI-based ES feedback, and ES coupled
with convolutional neural networks for adaptive latent space
tuning.
Both of the ES approaches have proven to be useful for an
extremely large number of applications, as described below, and
for an extremely large family of dynamic systems, as described
above. Each family of ES approaches also has its own benefits. The
family of classical ES-based optimizers is older, larger, and has
many more variants. On the other hand the smaller family of ESbased feedbacks has its own advantage in the increased flexibility

Alexander Scheinker received undergraduate degrees
in Mathematics and Physics from Washington University in St. Louis in 2006 and a M.S. in Mathematics
and a Ph.D. in control theory from the University of
California, San Diego in 2008 and 2012, respectively. He
has been a staff researcher at Los Alamos National Laboratory since 2011 where he is the Adaptive Machine
Learning team leader in the Applied Electrodynamics
group. His research focus is on applying advanced
control theory and machine learning methods to electrodynamics and for the stabilization and optimization
of noisy and analytically unknown complex time-varying systems. He has been
developing ES algorithms, proving their stability properties, and applying them
for non-invasive diagnostics and for the control of intense charged particle
beams in large particle accelerator facilities. Recently he has been focused on
combining ES with deep learning methods such as 3D convolutional neural
networks, to develop AI tools that are robust for time-varying systems with
distribution shift. Alexander has coauthored over 20 journal papers on extremum seeking theory and applications and the book Model-Free Stabilization
by Extremum Seeking.
